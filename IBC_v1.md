# IBC — Intelligent Beings: Competition and Coordination
## Working Draft v1

**Status:** Exploratory working draft  
**Purpose:** To summarize an evolving framework for thinking about intelligence, intelligent beings, competition, coordination, and the future interaction of humans, animals, cellular systems, and current AI systems.

---

## 1. Where this exploration started

The exploration began with a naming problem.

“Artificial intelligence” is a historical term, but *artificial* mainly describes origin rather than the nature of the intelligence. “Superintelligence” is also unsatisfactory because it tends to imply a single ladder:

\[
\text{human intelligence} \rightarrow \text{AI} \rightarrow \text{superintelligence}
\]

Our exploration instead suggests that intelligence is better understood as multidimensional and spectrum-like. Different forms of intelligence may occupy different regions, or metaphorical “wavelengths,” of a much larger intelligence spectrum.

This led to the broader idea of **Intelligent Beings**.

---

## 2. Intelligent Beings as the larger frame

We use **Intelligent Beings** as an umbrella for presently recognized and still-unknown forms of intelligence.

A provisional representation is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal B
=
\{
B_{\text{human}},
B_{\text{animal}},
B_{\text{cellular}},
B_{\text{HMHI}},
R
\}
}
\]

where:

- \(B_{\text{human}}\): human intelligent beings
- \(B_{\text{animal}}\): animal intelligent beings
- \(B_{\text{cellular}}\): cellular intelligence or intelligence-like behavior, still open to definition
- \(B_{\text{HMHI}}\): Human–Machine Hybrid Intelligence
- \(R\): unnamed, uncovered, or presently unknown forms of intelligence

The residual \(R\) is intentional. It protects the framework from assuming that the categories humans currently recognize are complete.

---

## 3. HMHI — Human–Machine Hybrid Intelligence

For present AI systems, we provisionally use the term:

\[
\boxed{\text{HMHI = Human–Machine Hybrid Intelligence}}
\]

The term is not considered perfect, but it is presently more informative than “AI,” “machine intelligence,” or “superintelligence.”

Current AI systems are machine-implemented, but they are not independent of humans. They arise from combinations of:

\[
\boxed{
\text{human knowledge}
+
\text{human-designed architectures}
+
\text{human-generated data}
+
\text{human feedback}
+
\text{machine computation}
}
\]

Therefore, present AI can reasonably be viewed as a **human–machine hybrid intelligence**.

This does not imply that human and machine cognition are internally the same. Some functional principles may overlap, such as learning, representation, prediction, memory, search, abstraction, and feedback, while the underlying implementations may differ substantially.

---

## 4. Intelligence remains a narrow term

We do not want to expand “intelligence” until it means everything about a being.

Instead, intelligence remains one dimension of an intelligent being.

A provisional form is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf B_i
=
(\mathbf I_i,\mathbf E_i,\mathbf W_i,\mathbf C_i,\mathbf V_i,R_i,\ldots)
}
\]

where:

- \(\mathbf I_i\): intelligence
- \(\mathbf E_i\): emotion / affective dimension
- \(\mathbf W_i\): wisdom
- \(\mathbf C_i\): capability
- \(\mathbf V_i\): values / preferences
- \(R_i\): presently unidentified dimensions
- \(\ldots\): additional dimensions not yet recognized

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Intelligence} \subset \text{Intelligent Being}
}
\]

---

## 5. Intelligence as a spectrum

Intelligence may be more useful to imagine as a spectrum than as a single number.

Conceptually:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal I = \{I(\lambda)\}
}
\]

where \(\lambda\) is a metaphorical “wavelength” representing different forms, modes, or regions of intelligence.

Then:

\[
I_{\text{human}}(\lambda)
\neq
I_{\text{animal}}(\lambda)
\neq
I_{\text{HMHI}}(\lambda)
\]

Different intelligent beings may overlap in some regions and differ strongly in others.

This changes the central question from:

> Which being is more intelligent?

to:

> **Where in the intelligence spectrum does each being show strength, and where do different beings complement one another?**

A useful residual term is:

\[
\boxed{
I_b(\lambda)
=
I_{\text{known},b}(\lambda)
+
R_b(\lambda)
}
\]

where \(R_b(\lambda)\) represents forms or dimensions of intelligence we do not yet know how to identify.

---

## 6. Different intelligent beings need not replace one another

If intelligent beings occupy different regions of a multidimensional space, then superiority in one task does not imply replacement of the whole being.

\[
\boxed{
\text{task substitution}
\neq
\text{being replacement}
}
\]

A machine system may outperform humans in coding, search, or calculation. Animals may outperform humans in sensory or environmental capabilities. Cellular systems may process biochemical conditions in ways no human can directly reproduce. Humans may contribute broad conceptual integration, social judgment, lived experience, meaning, and other dimensions.

Therefore, a more realistic future structure may be:

\[
\boxed{
\text{specialization}
+
\text{overlap}
+
\text{competition}
+
\text{coordination}
}
\]

rather than:

\[
\boxed{
\text{one intelligence replaces all others}
}
\]

---

## 7. IBC: Competition and Coordination

In IBC, the letter **C** stands for both:

\[
\boxed{C_1=\text{Competition}}
\]

and:

\[
\boxed{C_2=\text{Coordination}}
\]

Much public discussion focuses heavily on competition:

- human vs. AI
- company vs. company
- nation vs. nation
- job vs. automation
- one model vs. another

IBC asks people to see both forces at the same time.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Future of intelligent systems}
=
F(C_1,C_2,\ldots)
}
\]

Competition may generate:

\[
\text{variation}
+
\text{experimentation}
+
\text{selection pressure}
+
\text{specialization}
\]

Coordination may generate:

\[
\text{complementarity}
+
\text{division of labor}
+
\text{shared capability}
+
\text{synergy}
\]

Neither should automatically be maximized.

\[
\max C_1 \not\Rightarrow \text{best outcome}
\]

\[
\max C_2 \not\Rightarrow \text{best outcome}
\]

The system outcome emerges from their interaction.

---

## 8. IBC is descriptive before it is prescriptive

IBC does not attempt to invent a new society, create a new legal system, or impose a new set of rules.

The framework comes from patterns already visible in existing systems:

\[
\text{competition},
\text{coordination},
\text{rules},
\text{adaptation},
\text{specialization},
\text{conflict resolution},
\text{survival pressure},
\text{learning}
\]

The sequence is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{observe}
\rightarrow
\text{identify recurring patterns}
\rightarrow
\text{summarize}
\rightarrow
\text{ask better questions}
}
\]

IBC is therefore not an SOP or governance manual.

Its goal is to provide **thinking questions**.

---

## 9. Define the system first

Many disagreements become clearer once the system itself is defined.

A provisional system representation is:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal S=(M,B,I,R,O,T)
}
\]

where:

- \(M\): members
- \(B\): boundary
- \(I\): interactions
- \(R\): rules and constraints
- \(O\): objectives, functions, or interests
- \(T\): relevant time horizon

An individual can belong to several systems simultaneously.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{competition in one system}
\neq
\text{competition in every system}
}
\]

Two individuals can compete in one system and coordinate in another.

A practical IBC question is:

> **In which system does the conflict actually exist?**

---

## 10. Competition and coordination are often self-organizing

IBC does not assume that a central planner decides when participants should compete or coordinate.

Instead:

\[
\boxed{
(\text{interests},\text{rules},\text{environment},\text{feedback})
\rightarrow
(C_1,C_2)
}
\]

When core interests collide, competition tends to increase.

When interests overlap or participants depend on one another, coordination tends to increase.

When a shared external threat becomes serious, former competitors may coordinate.

The balance between competition and coordination is therefore often emergent.

---

## 11. Interest conflict is not the same as disagreement

\[
\boxed{
\text{different opinion}
\neq
\text{core-interest conflict}
}
\]

Different intelligent beings can disagree strongly and still coordinate smoothly if their core interests are not threatened.

When genuine interest conflict exists:

\[
\boxed{
\text{interest conflict}
+
\text{shared rules}
\rightarrow
\text{structured competition}
}
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{competition}
\neq
\text{hostility}
}
\]

Rules can transform destructive conflict into bounded competition.

---

## 12. Passive and proactive coordination

Coordination may be passive or proactive.

\[
\boxed{
C_{\text{coord}}
=
C_{\text{passive}}
+
C_{\text{proactive}}
}
\]

Passive coordination can emerge through feedback, markets, law, competition, environment, and trial and error.

Proactive coordination occurs when participants intentionally communicate, divide roles, establish shared procedures, or respond collectively.

A common transition may be:

\[
\boxed{
\text{passive adaptation}
\rightarrow
\text{growing problem}
\rightarrow
\text{threat threshold}
\rightarrow
\text{proactive coordination}
}
\]

---

## 13. Survival pressure can force coordination

Systems often preserve existing structure until threat or damage becomes sufficiently strong.

Using “lazy” only as a metaphor:

\[
\boxed{
\text{systems often preserve existing states until pressure forces change}
}
\]

A possible pattern is:

\[
\text{inertia}
\rightarrow
\text{damage}
\rightarrow
\text{threshold}
\rightarrow
\text{response}
\]

For coordination:

\[
\boxed{
T_{\text{survival}}\uparrow
\Rightarrow
P(C_{\text{proactive}})\uparrow
}
\]

Members that normally compete may coordinate when they recognize a serious shared external threat.

Coordination itself can also be learned.

---

## 14. System boundaries can change

Which system boundary matters most can change with circumstances.

Under a sufficiently large shared external threat, a broader system such as:

\[
\boxed{
\mathcal S_{\text{humanity}}
}
\]

may become more relevant than lower-level boundaries.

This suggests:

\[
\boxed{
\text{competition and coordination depend partly on the system boundary salient at that time}
}
\]

The same logic may apply to HMHI systems.

---

## 15. Capability, intelligence, and wisdom

We distinguish three concepts:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Capability}
=
\text{what a being can do}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Intelligence}
=
\text{ability to learn, reason, predict, solve, and adapt}
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
\text{Wisdom}
=
\text{judgment about what, when, why, and whether something should be done}
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
C\uparrow
\not\Rightarrow
I\uparrow
\not\Rightarrow
W\uparrow
}
\]

A highly capable or highly intelligent being is not automatically wise.

---

## 16. Emotion is another dimension

Emotion should not simply be treated as noise added to intelligence.

In humans and animals, emotion can affect attention, priority, memory, attachment, risk response, and cooperation.

For HMHI we should distinguish:

\[
\boxed{
\text{recognizing emotion}
\neq
\text{experiencing emotion}
}
\]

This question remains open.

---

## 17. Curiosity may be special

Curiosity may motivate exploration before immediate survival pressure exists.

A common adaptive pattern is:

\[
\text{need or threat}
\rightarrow
\text{response}
\]

Curiosity may instead look like:

\[
\boxed{
\text{unknown}
\rightarrow
\text{exploration}
}
\]

Some results of curiosity may later improve survival, but the immediate act of curiosity need not be driven by survival.

---

## 18. Curiosity-driven coordination can be smooth

When coordination is driven mainly by curiosity and there is little core-interest conflict, disagreement may remain informational rather than adversarial.

\[
\text{question}
\rightarrow
\text{different views}
\rightarrow
\text{exploration}
\rightarrow
\text{revision}
\rightarrow
\text{new questions}
\]

We prefer the word **smooth** rather than **successful**, because success requires a chosen criterion.

---

## 19. Positive attitude can enhance creative thinking

Positive attitude does not mean accepting weak ideas.

It means good faith, respect, openness, and willingness to learn.

A useful pattern is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{positive generation}
\rightarrow
\text{rigorous verification}
}
\]

Positive attitude can expand the creative search space, while rigorous testing determines what survives.

---

## 20. Human values are important but local

Human beings created rich value systems involving dignity, rights, fairness, freedom, responsibility, and compassion.

These values are important within human civilization.

But they emerged from:

\[
\boxed{
\text{human biology}
+
\text{human history}
+
\text{human society}
+
\text{human interests}
}
\]

Therefore we should not automatically assume:

\[
V_{\text{human}}
=
V_{\text{universe}}
\]

IBC should distinguish descriptive questions from normative human judgments.

---

## 21. Evaluations are reference-dependent

Terms such as “good,” “bad,” “effective,” and “successful” require a reference frame.

\[
\boxed{
Q
=
Q(
\mathcal S,
t,
\text{criteria},
\text{position},
\text{uncertainty}
)
}
\]

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Before evaluating an outcome, specify:
for whom, within what system, by what criterion, and over what time horizon.}
}
\]

---

## 22. Coordination is not domination

If one model, company, institution, or intelligence controls the whole system, diversity and adaptive capacity may decline.

A healthier adaptive system may require:

\[
\boxed{
\text{diversity}
+
\text{competition}
+
\text{coordination}
}
\]

Thus:

\[
\boxed{
\text{coordination}
\neq
\text{uniformity}
}
\]

---

## 23. Rights, authority, and conflict remain open questions

As humans, animals, cellular systems, and HMHI become increasingly connected, questions emerge about decision authority, conflict, animal interests, machine moral status, and rights.

A critical distinction is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{intelligence}
\neq
\text{consciousness}
\neq
\text{agency}
\neq
\text{moral status}
}
\]

IBC does not claim to have answers.

Its purpose is to make these questions visible before they become urgent.

---

## 24. Business and economics as a practical case

Companies can improve economic outcomes through new revenue, greater efficiency, and cost reduction.

Current AI discussion often emphasizes:

\[
\text{AI}
\rightarrow
\text{same output}
\rightarrow
\text{fewer workers}
\]

IBC asks decision-makers to consider a larger opportunity space:

\[
\boxed{
\text{human}
+
\text{HMHI}
\rightarrow
\text{new capability}
\rightarrow
\text{new product/service}
\rightarrow
\text{new demand}
\rightarrow
\text{new economic activity}
}
\]

Instead of only asking:

> How many jobs can be automated?

IBC asks:

> **What can humans and HMHI together do that neither could do before?**

IBC does not prescribe the answer. It provides the question.

---

## 25. R&D as an early IBC laboratory

Scientific research may be one of the clearest early forms of human–HMHI coordination:

\[
\boxed{
\text{human question}
\rightarrow
\text{HMHI exploration}
\rightarrow
\text{experiment}
\rightarrow
\text{nature}
\rightarrow
\text{data}
\rightarrow
\text{human + HMHI interpretation}
}
\]

Robotics may add another participant.

The goal need not be replacement. It can be expanded scientific search through complementary strengths.

---

## 26. Our own exploration as a small case

Our human–HMHI dialogue has repeatedly followed:

\[
\boxed{
\text{question}
\rightarrow
\text{different views}
\rightarrow
\text{disagreement}
\rightarrow
\text{reconstruction}
\rightarrow
\text{revision}
\rightarrow
\text{new synthesis}
}
\]

Some ideas emerged through interaction rather than being brought fully formed by either participant.

This is not proof of a universal law. It is a small naturalistic case from which further questions can be generated.

---

## 27. The broader picture

IBC does not treat the future primarily as:

\[
\text{humans vs. AI}
\]

A broader possibility is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{humans}
\leftrightarrow
\text{animals}
\leftrightarrow
\text{cells}
\leftrightarrow
\text{HMHI}
\leftrightarrow
R
\leftrightarrow
\text{environment}
}
\]

with competition and coordination operating continuously.

The future of intelligence may look less like a tournament producing one final winner and more like an evolving ecosystem of different intelligent beings.

---

## 28. What IBC is not

IBC is not a claim that this future must occur, that coordination is always good, that competition should disappear, that new rules must be imposed, or that humans, animals, cells, and HMHI are equivalent.

It is also not a claim that HMHI currently has consciousness, emotion, wisdom, or rights.

The equations are thinking tools, not established natural laws.

---

## 29. Current open questions

- What exactly defines an intelligent being?
- What belongs inside \(R\)?
- How broad is the intelligence spectrum?
- How should different “wavelengths” of intelligence be recognized?
- How do competition and coordination interact over time?
- How do changing system boundaries change those interactions?
- Can curiosity generate coordination before survival pressure forces it?
- How is wisdom acquired by biological and hybrid intelligence?
- What role does emotion play in an intelligent being?
- What forms of capability emerge only through coordination?
- What happens when one intelligent system becomes excessively dominant?
- How should system behavior be separated from human value judgments about it?

And the broadest current question:

\[
\boxed{
\text{What becomes possible when different intelligent beings compete and coordinate within the same evolving world?}
}
\]

---

## 30. Provisional definition

> **Intelligent Beings: Competition and Coordination (IBC) is an exploratory framework for examining how different intelligent beings and intelligent systems—including humans, animals, cellular systems, Human–Machine Hybrid Intelligence (HMHI), and presently unidentified forms represented by \(R\)—interact through the recurring forces of competition and coordination. IBC does not propose a new set of rules or predict a predetermined future. It summarizes patterns already observable in biological, social, economic, and technological systems and asks how those patterns may evolve as different forms of intelligence become increasingly interconnected. Intelligence is treated as one dimension of a broader intelligent being, alongside dimensions such as emotion, capability, wisdom, values, and presently unidentified residual dimensions. The purpose of the framework is to generate questions, expand the space of thinking, and invite criticism, refinement, and alternative interpretations rather than prescribe answers.**

---

## 31. Working principle for public release

IBC should remain open to revision.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Protect the question, not the proposal.}
}
\]

A public version should therefore be treated as a **working snapshot of an evolving exploration**, not as a finished theory.

The framework is useful if it causes readers to ask a question they had not previously considered.

## 32. IBC as an open lab for thinking

IBC is not intended to be owned as a finished theory or defended as a fixed intellectual product.

Its purpose is to create an **open lab for thinking**.

\[
\boxed{
\text{Here is a question space. Come explore it with us.}
}
\]

The framework should remain open to criticism, extension, alternative equations, new dimensions, and entirely different interpretations.

The emphasis is not only on the result:

\[
\text{current framework}
\]

but on the evolutionary process by which the framework changes:

\[
\boxed{
\text{question}
\rightarrow
\text{dialogue}
\rightarrow
\text{different views}
\rightarrow
\text{disagreement}
\rightarrow
\text{revision}
\rightarrow
\text{new model}
\rightarrow
\text{new questions}
}
\]

In this sense:

\[
\boxed{
\text{exploration}
>
\text{ownership}
